# How to Use ChatGPT for Lesson Planning

It's Sunday night. You've got five lessons to plan for Monday, a stack of ungraded quizzes, and a lingering feeling that the unit you taught last week didn't quite land. If this sounds familiar, you're not alone — teachers routinely report spending 7-10 hours a week on planning alone. ChatGPT won't replace your judgment about what your students need, but used well, it can take a real chunk of that time back.

Here's how to actually use it — not as a magic lesson-generator, but as a thinking partner that speeds up the parts of planning that eat your time without adding much value.

## 1. Start With Standards, Not Topics

The biggest mistake educators make is asking ChatGPT for "a lesson on photosynthesis" and hoping for the best. Instead, feed it the actual standard you're teaching to.

**Try this prompt:**
> "I'm teaching NGSS MS-LS1-6 (photosynthesis and cellular respiration) to 7th graders. Give me three different lesson approaches: one hands-on/lab-based, one discussion-based, and one that uses a real-world case study. For each, include the core concept, a 5-minute hook, and one formative assessment question."

This gives you options to choose from rather than one generic output, and it stays anchored to what you're actually required to teach.

## 2. Use It to Differentiate, Not Just Generate

Where ChatGPT saves the most time is in creating variations of the same lesson for different learners — something that's often skipped entirely because it's so time-consuming to do manually.

Ask for:
- A simplified version of a reading passage for students below grade level
- An extension activity for students who finish early
- Sentence starters or vocabulary scaffolds for English language learners

One middle school science teacher we spoke with described cutting her differentiation prep from roughly 45 minutes to under 10 minutes per lesson by asking ChatGPT to take her existing worksheet and generate a modified version, rather than starting from scratch each time.

## 3. Draft Assessments, Then Edit Ruthlessly

ChatGPT is genuinely good at generating a first draft of quiz questions, exit tickets, or rubrics — but it will sometimes produce questions that are ambiguous, too easy, or not aligned to what you actually taught. Treat its output as a rough draft, not a final product.

**Workflow that works:**
1. Ask for 10 questions at a range of difficulty levels
2. Read through and cut anything ambiguous or off-target
3. Ask it to revise the ones you flagged, telling it specifically what was wrong

This is faster than writing from scratch, but still keeps you as the final quality check — which matters, since ChatGPT has no idea what you actually covered in class today.

## 4. Build a Reusable Prompt Library

Instead of re-explaining your grade level, subject, and teaching style every time, save prompts that already work for you. For example:

> "You are helping a 9th grade world history teacher plan lessons. Lessons should include a warm-up question, a primary source excerpt, three discussion questions, and a short writing prompt. Keep reading levels appropriate for 14-15 year olds."

Reuse this as your starting template for every lesson in the unit, swapping only the topic and source material. This alone can cut your prompting time by more than half.

## 5. Know Where to Stop

ChatGPT doesn't know your students, your classroom dynamics, or what happened in yesterday's class. It's best for:
- First drafts of lessons, worksheets, and assessments
- Generating variations and differentiated materials
- Brainstorming discussion questions or hooks

It's not a substitute for your judgment on pacing, classroom management, or reading the room. The teachers who get the most value treat it like a fast, tireless teaching assistant — not an autopilot.

## Try It This Week

Pick one lesson you're planning this week and run it through the standards-first prompt above. Compare the time it takes against your usual planning process, and see what you'd still want to change. Small tests like this are the fastest way to find out where AI tools actually fit into your workflow — and where they don't.
